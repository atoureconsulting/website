The Story

The most trusted ingredient in Black beauty.
Almost nobody knows where it comes from.

There is no need to invent a homecoming. The connection is already there, and it is stronger than ancestry because it is still happening. This journey asks a simple question that carries a whole country inside it: where does it actually come from, and who makes it?

There

A jar everybody knows

Shea butter has been in Black homes for generations. On hair, on skin, on newborns, passed from mothers to daughters long before any company put it in a bottle. It is the one ingredient nobody has to be sold on. Taraji P. Henson has spent a career inside the industries that carry it: film, television, fashion and beauty.

Here

The hands that make it

It begins in the northern savannah of Côte d'Ivoire. Women walk out to collect the nuts, then sort, roast, grind, knead and extract, almost entirely by hand. Shea is one of the country's major agricultural exports and roughly nine in ten producers are women. West Africa already has a name for it that says everything: women's gold.

Why her

She has made this her subject before

Taraji built one of her defining performances on Katherine Johnson, a Black woman whose essential work went uncredited for decades. She is the actress most associated with the argument that women's invisible labour should be made visible. This country runs an entire industry on exactly that.

She has spent her life inside an industry built on this country's work. She has never met the women who do it. That is not a holiday. That is a film.

The Timing Consideration

This story has a season.
Most projects do not.

Shea is not available to film on demand. Collection and traditional processing in northern Côte d'Ivoire follow an agricultural calendar, and the work that makes this documentary worth watching, women walking out to collect, sorting, roasting, grinding, kneading and extracting, only happens when the season allows it.

Arrive outside that window and the centrepiece of the film becomes a demonstration staged for a camera. Every viewer can feel the difference, and so can every sponsor. This single constraint should drive the date, not the other way around.

AToure will confirm the current season window directly with cooperative leadership and regional agricultural contacts in the Poro region before any date is proposed. It is the kind of detail that decides whether this film is real, and it is not something a partner working remotely will catch.

Transport & domestic aviation

Premium vehicles with drivers across Abidjan and Grand Bassam, plus the Abidjan to Korhogo leg by scheduled domestic service or charter, planned around crew, equipment and the return to the capital for the cover production.

05

Hospitality

Five star suite and team accommodation in Abidjan, and properly reconnoitred accommodation in Korhogo, where the hospitality standard is different and needs to be inspected in person rather than booked online.

06

Permits, consent & media

Filming and equipment permits across every jurisdiction on the route, plus the community consent architecture for the cooperative: cooperative agreement, translation, individual releases and a clear position on how participants are compensated and credited.
