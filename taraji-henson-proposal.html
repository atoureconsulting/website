Theme Two · Karité & Natural Cosmetics

An industry built by women.
Owned by other people.

Shea butter has been in Black homes for generations. On hair, on skin, on newborns, passed from mothers to daughters long before any company put it in a bottle. In West Africa it already has a name that says everything: women's gold.

It begins in the northern savannah. Women walk out to collect the nuts, then sort, roast, grind, knead and extract, almost entirely by hand. The finished butter travels to Paris, London and Los Angeles and multiplies in value on the way. Very little of that increase comes back to Korhogo.

The same story is now being rewritten closer to home. A generation of Ivorian women are building natural cosmetics brands in Abidjan, buying from the cooperatives and finishing the product in the country. That is the part the world has not seen, and it is the part worth filming.

This chapter is not about scarcity. It is about value, ownership and expertise, and about women at both ends of a supply chain recognising each other as people who do the same work.

Why This Is Personal For Her

She built a company
to protect what was under the wig.

I understood very young that your scalp is your foundation. It needs to be healthy in order for your hair to grow.
Taraji P. Henson, on why she created TPH by Taraji

TPH by Taraji started because she could not find anything to look after her own scalp under the protective styles a film career required, and she spent years mixing small batches in her kitchen before it became a company. In April 2025 she went further and bought the brand back outright, taking full ownership from the group that had backed it since 2020. She has personally lived the exact economics this chapter is built on: build something real, watch someone else hold the value for a while, then fight to own it outright. That is not a theme she is being asked to perform for the cameras. It is one she has already been through.

Why This Is Personal For Her

She has cried about this
on the record.

I'm only human and it seems every time I do something, and I break another glass ceiling, when it's time to renegotiate I'm at the bottom again like I never did what I just did. And I'm just tired.
Taraji P. Henson, SiriusXM interview, December 2023

In one widely reported case she asked for 500,000 dollars for a film role and settled for 150,000. After tax and her team's share, she kept roughly 40,000 of it. She has since restructured her own team so her success actually reaches her rather than passing through others on its way to her. That is the same conversation a shea cooperative in Korhogo will have on camera: work that visibly creates value, and a fight to keep a fair share of it. She is not narrating that gap as an observer. She is one of the people living it.

Sources: Variety, 2019. Forbes, December 2023.
